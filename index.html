Function Key

Inner-product functional encryption · DDH (ABDP15) · ristretto255

Encrypt a vector, then open it two ways — an ordinary decryption that hands back the whole vector, and a functional key for y that hands back only <x, y> — and watch where the second one runs out of room and what happens once you hold enough of them.

WHY IT MATTERS

Releasing a statistic without releasing the records is the shape of almost every real data-sharing request, and functional encryption is the version of that with a proof attached rather than a promise. The catch is worth knowing before you rely on it: the authority that issues those keys is deciding, one key at a time, how much of the plaintext it has already given away — and there is a point past which it has given away everything.

What is this?

Normal encryption is all-or-nothing: either you hold the key and see the whole message, or you do not and see nothing. Functional encryption adds a middle setting. The authority can issue a key that answers exactly one question about the encrypted data and nothing else.

The question here is a weighted sum. If the encrypted vector x is a row of numbers — say a patient's lab values, or a term's coursework marks — then a key for the weight vector y returns the single number <x, y> = x₁y₁ + x₂y₂ + …, the weighted total. The key holder learns the average, or the score, or the subtotal. They do not learn the row.

That much is the good news, and it is real — the scheme on this page is the published one and the arithmetic is genuine. The rest of the page is about the two places it stops being that tidy, both of which you can run yourself here rather than take on trust.
